# Talent & training

**How the firm builds the fluency to use any of this safely — the tiered curriculum, the regulatory duty behind it, and why proficiency, not tooling, is the real adoption strategy.**

The roles are named and the operating model is built. Whether any of it works now depends on the workforce. Put capable tools in front of people who have not been taught to use them and one of two things happens: they ignore the tools, and the investment returns nothing, or they use them badly on real client data, and the investment returns an incident. Both outcomes are failures of training, not technology, and both are leadership's to prevent.

## A tiered curriculum, not a single course

Training fails when it is one generic course pushed at everyone. The workforce does not have one need; it has four, and they layer. A serious program is tiered.

**All-staff AI literacy** is the base, and everyone gets it: what these tools are and, just as important, what they cannot do; how to use them safely; data hygiene — what may and may not be pasted into which tool; and when to escalate rather than trust an answer. This tier is as much risk control as capability building. A workforce that does not know what *not* to do with AI is a workforce quietly creating incidents.

**Power users** are the larger population who work with AI daily without building it. Their training is craft: how to prompt well, how to verify output rather than accept it, and how to collaborate with an agent — knowing which part of the work to hand over and which to keep. This tier is where most of the day-to-day productivity actually comes from.

**Builders** are the engineers and analysts who construct agents and pipelines. They need the SDKs and agent frameworks, but more than that they need evaluation discipline and governance-by-design — the habit of building the audit log and the shadow path in from the start, not bolting them on after a demo impresses someone. The companion repositories beneath this guide are the hands-on curriculum for this tier.

**Leaders** need their own training, and it is the tier firms most often skip. Executives and risk owners do not need to write prompts; they need to read a scorecard, understand what a model-risk finding means, ask the right question in an oversight meeting, and know where their accountability begins and ends. A leadership team that cannot interpret the evidence its own governance produces cannot govern.

## The regulatory duty behind literacy

There is now regulatory weight behind all of this, and it is worth naming precisely. The Department of Labor has issued guidance **encouraging** employers to provide AI-literacy training — encouragement, not yet a mandate, but a clear signal of where expectations are heading. Under the **EU AI Act, Article 4** goes further: it places an actual duty on providers and deployers to ensure their people have a sufficient level of AI literacy. For any firm with an EU nexus — clients, staff, or vendors — training is not merely good practice; it is a compliance obligation with a named article behind it. The emerging norm is a multi-segment program on a roughly two-year horizon, not a one-time rollout, because fluency at four tiers is not built in an afternoon.

## Proficiency is the adoption strategy

The uncomfortable finding from firms ahead on this curve is that adoption tracks proficiency far more than it tracks tool quality. People use what they are good at and avoid what makes them feel slow. That reframes training from a cost center into the primary lever for return on the whole AI investment — the tools deliver value only at the rate people become fluent with them.

Two forces accelerate that. The first is **visible executive use**: when leaders demonstrably use these tools in their own work, the signal that this is how the firm operates now lands harder than any memo. The second is **incentives** — the same hardwiring the C-suite committed to, extended down the organization.

## Hiring, promotion, and pay carry the signal

Culture follows the reward system, so AI fluency has to show up in it. In **hiring**, screen for it — not deep technical skill for every role, but the judgment to use these tools well and safely, and the disposition to keep learning as they change. In **promotion**, treat fluency and safe use as part of what advancement requires, the same way client outcomes or risk discipline already are; a leader who cannot read the evidence their governance produces is not ready to own it. In **pay**, reflect it — modestly and honestly, tied to real capability rather than badges collected. None of this is a slogan. It is a set of line items people are actually assessed against, and it is what tells the organization the firm means what its strategy said.

## The cost of not doing it has a name

There is a cost to *not* training, and it has a name: **shadow AI.** Staff denied sanctioned, well-governed tools and the training to use them do not stop using AI — they use it anyway, on personal accounts, outside every control, pasting client information into whatever consumer tool is at hand. Shadow AI is the direct risk of not training, and it is worse than the thing training was meant to avoid, because now the firm has all of the exposure and none of the visibility. The choice was never whether the workforce uses AI. It is only whether they use it where the firm can see it, governed and taught — or in the dark.
